Part A ??Why & Formulas
A1. Why polar is bad for squares, ideal for circles
Why are polar coords inefficient for a square's corners but ideal for a circle's points?
Conceptual Coordinate System Choice
A polar point is described by one angle + one distance from the pole ??exactly the two quantities that stay constant or change predictably on a circle (distance fixed, angle sweeps).
On a square, both the distance to the center and the direction vary non-smoothly as you walk along the edges. Each corner has the same distance to the center as some other corner but a totally different angle, and the side lengths require ugly mixed expressions in \(r\) and \(\theta\) (a square has no clean polar equation). Rectangular \((x,y)\) is the natural choice for grid-aligned shapes.
Polar = good for radial / circular motion; rect = good for grid shapes.
A2. Derive \(r^2\) and \(\tan\theta\)
From \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\), derive both \(r^2\) and \(\tan\theta\) in terms of \(x, y\).
Pythagorean Identity Derivation
For \(r^2\): Square and add: $$x^2 + y^2 = (r\cos\theta)^2 + (r\sin\theta)^2 = r^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r^2 \cdot 1 = r^2.$$ So \(\boxed{r^2 = x^2 + y^2.}\)
For \(\tan\theta\): Divide \(y\) by \(x\) (assuming \(r\cos\theta \neq 0\)): $$\frac{y}{x} = \frac{r\sin\theta}{r\cos\theta} = \tan\theta.$$ So \(\boxed{\tan\theta = \dfrac{y}{x}.}\)
\( r^2 = x^2+y^2,\quad \tan\theta = y/x \) (with quadrant check).
A3. TRUE / FALSE with justification
(a) \((r,\theta)\equiv(r,\theta+2\pi)\)   (b) \((-r,\theta)\equiv(r,\theta-\pi)\)   (c) \((0,\theta)\) is the pole for any \(\theta\)   (d) \((r,\theta)\equiv(-r,-\theta)\)
Polar Equivalence
(a) TRUE. Adding \(2\pi\) is a full rotation ??same ray, same distance.
(b) TRUE. Reflecting through the pole flips the radius sign and shifts the angle by \(\pi\) (either \(+\pi\) or \(-\pi\) works).
(c) TRUE. If \(r = 0\) the point is at distance \(0\) from the pole = the pole itself, regardless of \(\theta\).
(d) FALSE in general. Counter-example: \((r,\theta) = (1, \pi/4)\) sits at \((\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2})\). But \((-r,-\theta) = (-1, -\pi/4)\): angle \(-\pi/4\) points to \((\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2},-\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2})\); since \(r=-1\), flip to opposite side \(\Rightarrow (-\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2})\). Different from the original.
a-T, b-T, c-T, d-F

Part E ??Equation Conversion
E-Worked Example (convert \(y = x^2\) rect \(\to\) polar) ??solution shown in worksheet.
E1. Rect \(\to\) Polar: \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\)
Convert with \(r > 0\).
Circle Rect?뭁olar
Substitute \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\):
\(r^2 = 9 \;\Rightarrow\; r = 3\) (since \(r > 0\)).
\( r = 3 \) (circle of radius 3 centered at the pole)
E2. Rect \(\to\) Polar: \(x^2 - y^2 = 1\)
Use a double-angle identity.
Hyperbola Double-Angle
Substitute \(x = r\cos\theta,\;y = r\sin\theta\): $$(r\cos\theta)^2 - (r\sin\theta)^2 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; r^2(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta) = 1.$$
Use \(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = \cos 2\theta\): $$r^2\cos 2\theta = 1.$$
\( r^2 = \dfrac{1}{\cos 2\theta} = \sec 2\theta \) (defined where \(\cos 2\theta > 0\)).
E3. Polar \(\to\) Rect: \(r = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta - \cos\theta}\)
Convert to rectangular form.
Polar?뭃ect Line
Multiply both sides by \((\sin\theta - \cos\theta)\): $$r(\sin\theta - \cos\theta) = 1.$$
Distribute: $$r\sin\theta - r\cos\theta = 1.$$
Substitute \(r\sin\theta = y\) and \(r\cos\theta = x\): $$y - x = 1.$$
\( y = x + 1 \) (a line)
E4. Polar \(\to\) Rect: \(r^2 = \tan\theta\)
Convert to rectangular form. Verify the pole.
Polar?뭃ect Manipulation
Write \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\): $$r^2 = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}.$$
Multiply both sides by \(\cos\theta\): $$r^2 \cos\theta = \sin\theta.$$
Multiply both sides by \(r\) (introduces \(x\) and \(y\)): $$r^3 \cos\theta = r\sin\theta \;\Rightarrow\; r^2 \cdot (r\cos\theta) = (r\sin\theta) \;\Rightarrow\; (x^2 + y^2)\,x = y.$$
Final form: \((x^2+y^2)\,x = y,\;\) equivalently \(x^3 + xy^2 = y.\)
Verify pole: \((0,0)\) gives \(0 = 0\) ✓. The pole is on the curve, consistent with \(\theta=0,\;r=0\) in the original equation.
\( x^3 + xy^2 = y \) (equivalently \((x^2+y^2)x = y\))
