Polar Coordinates: Concepts, Conversion & Graph Sketching

Student: Albert Lee Date: 2026-05-24
Standard: CCSS.MATH.CONTENT.HSF.TF ??Trigonometric Functions in Polar Form. Convert between rectangular and polar coordinates; sketch polar equations using the Cartesian-trick.
Watch out for these (today's weak spots)
  1. Negative \(r\). A negative \(r\) means the point is on the opposite side of the pole.
  2. Angle unit = radians by default (calculus convention).
  3. arctan quadrant trap. \(\tan\theta = y/x\) alone does NOT pin down the quadrant. Always check the signs of \(x\) and \(y\) separately.
  4. Choosing \(r\). Take \(r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} > 0\) unless the problem allows \(r<0\).
  5. Multiplying by \(r\). When converting polar \(\to\) rectangular, multiplying both sides by \(r\) introduces the pole (origin) as an extra solution ??verify it satisfies the original equation.
Part A ??Why & Formulas
Directions: Show your reasoning. These check that the big idea is solid before you compute.
Note 쨌 Concept
Polar coordinates describe a point using one angle \(\theta\) and one distance \(r\) ??natural for circular motion, awkward for grid shapes. Conversion formulas: $$x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}$$ Trap: \(\tan\theta = y/x\) loses the quadrant. Always check signs of \(x\) and \(y\).
Worked Example ??Derive \(\tan\theta = y/x\)
Start. From \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\) (the two conversion formulas):
Step 1. Divide \(y\) by \(x\): $$\frac{y}{x} = \frac{r\sin\theta}{r\cos\theta}$$
Step 2. The \(r\) cancels (assuming \(r\neq 0\)), leaving $$\frac{y}{x} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \tan\theta. \quad\blacksquare$$
A1. Explain in 2–3 sentences why polar coordinates are inefficient for describing the four corners of a square but ideal for describing points on a circle.
A2. Given \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\), derive both \(r^2\) and \(\tan\theta\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\). Show every step (similar style to the worked example above).
A3. Mark each statement T or F and justify in one short sentence.
  1. T / F   \((r,\theta)\) and \((r,\theta+2\pi)\) are the same point.
  2. T / F   \((-r,\theta)\) and \((r,\theta-\pi)\) are the same point.
  3. T / F   \((0,\theta)\) is the pole for any value of \(\theta\).
  4. T / F   \((r,\theta)\) and \((-r,-\theta)\) are the same point.
Part B ??Plotting & Alternative Representations
Directions: Use a polar grid (sketch in the work space). Read the signs of \(r\) and \(\theta\) both carefully.
Note 쨌 Multiple labels
A polar point has infinitely many labels: $$(r,\theta) \;=\; (-r,\; \theta+\pi) \;=\; (r,\; \theta + 2\pi k), \quad k \in \mathbb{Z}.$$ Negative \(r\) reflects the point across the pole (origin).
Worked Example ??Plot \((-2,\;5\pi/6)\) and find two alternative reps
Plot. Rotate to \(\theta = 5\pi/6\) (a ray pointing into Q-II at 150째). Since \(r = -2\), the point lies on the opposite ray, i.e. in Q-IV at \(\theta = 5\pi/6 - \pi = -\pi/6\), distance 2 from the pole.

Rep #2 (\(r < 0\), different \(\theta\)). Add \(2\pi\) to the angle: \( \bigl(-2,\; 5\pi/6 + 2\pi\bigr) = \bigl(-2,\; 17\pi/6\bigr).\)

Part C ??Point Conversion
Directions: Reduce any over-rotated angles first. For rect \(\to\) polar, give \(r>0\) and \(0 \le \theta < 2\pi\). Quadrant check is required.
Note 쨌 Direction matters
Polar \(\to\) Rectangular: \(x = r\cos\theta,\;\; y = r\sin\theta\). Just substitute.
Rectangular \(\to\) Polar: \(r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\;\; \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\). Always check the quadrant from the signs of \(x\) and \(y\) before reporting \(\theta\) ??naive \(\arctan(y/x)\) loses Q-II and Q-III.
Worked Example ??Convert \((-3,\; 3\sqrt{3})\) rect \(\to\) polar (\(r>0,\;0\le\theta<2\pi\))
Quadrant. \(x = -3 < 0\), \(y = 3\sqrt{3} > 0\) \(\Rightarrow\) the point lies in Q-II.
Radius. \(r = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + (3\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{9 + 27} = \sqrt{36} = 6.\)
Reference angle. \(\tan\theta_{ref} = \left|\dfrac{y}{x}\right| = \dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{3} = \sqrt{3} \;\Rightarrow\; \theta_{ref} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}.\)
Quadrant adjust (Q-II). \(\theta = \pi - \dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}.\)
Answer. \(\bigl(r,\theta\bigr) = \bigl(6,\;\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\bigr).\)
C1. Polar \(\to\) Rectangular: \(\left(-4,\;\dfrac{23\pi}{4}\right).\) Hint: reduce \(\theta\) modulo \(2\pi\) first.
C2. Polar \(\to\) Rectangular: \(\left(6,\;\dfrac{11\pi}{6}\right).\)
C3. Rectangular \(\to\) Polar: \((-\sqrt{6},\;-\sqrt{2}).\) (Tricky — Q-III. Exact form, \(r>0,\;0\le\theta<2\pi\).)
C4. Rectangular \(\to\) Polar: \((0,\,-5).\) (Edge case — on the negative \(y\)-axis. State \(r\) and \(\theta\) directly without dividing by zero.)
Part D ??Sketching \(r = a\cos\theta\) / \(r = a\sin\theta\)
Directions: Use the Cartesian-trick. Sketch the sinusoid first; then read its height as \(r\) and convert to a polar point.
Note 쨌 The Cartesian-trick
Step 1. First sketch the Cartesian \(y = a\cos x\) (or \(y = a\sin x\)).
Step 2. Treat the input \(x\) as the polar angle \(\theta\). The output \(y\) becomes the polar radius \(r\) for that angle.
Step 3. When \(y < 0\) (i.e. \(r < 0\)), the polar point lies on the opposite ray. Don't worry — the curve still ends up on the same circle.
Result. \(r = a\cos\theta\) is a circle of diameter \(|a|\) sitting on the \(x\)-axis. \(r = a\sin\theta\) is a circle of diameter \(|a|\) sitting on the \(y\)-axis. Sign of \(a\) flips the side.
Worked Example ??Sketch \(r = -2\cos\theta\)
Step 1. Sketch \(y = -2\cos x\) on Cartesian axes. (A flipped cosine: max \(+2\) at \(x = \pi\); min \(-2\) at \(x = 0\); zeros at \(x = \pi/2,\;3\pi/2\).)
Step 2. Read off values:
  • At \(\theta = 0\): \(r = -2\) → opposite side, distance 2 from pole → point \((-2, 0)\) in rect.
  • At \(\theta = \pi/2\): \(r = 0\) → the pole.
  • At \(\theta = \pi\): \(r = 2\) → angle 180째, distance 2 → point \((-2, 0)\) again.
  • At \(\theta = 3\pi/2\): \(r = 0\) → pole.
Step 3. Connect smoothly → circle of diameter 2 lying entirely on the negative \(x\)-axis (center \((-1, 0)\), radius 1). The whole circle is traced over \(\theta \in [0,\pi]\); \(\theta \in [\pi, 2\pi]\) retraces it.

Part E ??Equation Conversion
Directions: Show the substitution step before simplifying. State the final form clearly.
Note 쨌 Substitution rules
Rect \(\to\) Polar: sub \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\), then simplify.
Polar \(\to\) Rect: often it helps to multiply both sides by \(r\); then use \(r^2 = x^2+y^2,\; r\cos\theta = x,\; r\sin\theta = y\). Verify that the pole (origin) is consistent if you multiplied by \(r\).
Worked Example ??Convert \(y = x^2\) (rect) to polar
Sub. \(r\sin\theta = (r\cos\theta)^2 = r^2\cos^2\theta.\)
Divide by \(r\) (assuming \(r\neq 0\)): \(\sin\theta = r\cos^2\theta.\)
Solve for \(r\): \(r = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos^2\theta} = \sec\theta\,\tan\theta.\) The pole \((r=0)\) corresponds to \(\theta = 0\) (since \(\sin 0 = 0\)), which is the origin — on the parabola. Consistent.
E1. Rect \(\to\) Polar: \(x^2 + y^2 = 9.\) Give the answer with \(r > 0\).
E2. Rect \(\to\) Polar: \(x^2 - y^2 = 1.\) (A hyperbola — use a double-angle identity to compact the answer.)
E3. Polar \(\to\) Rect: \( r = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta - \cos\theta}.\) Hint: multiply both sides by \((\sin\theta - \cos\theta)\), then by \(r\) if useful.
E4. Polar \(\to\) Rect: \( r^2 = \tan\theta.\) Hint: write \(\tan\theta = \sin\theta / \cos\theta\), then multiply both sides by \(r\,\cos\theta\) to introduce \(x\) and \(y\). Verify the pole.
