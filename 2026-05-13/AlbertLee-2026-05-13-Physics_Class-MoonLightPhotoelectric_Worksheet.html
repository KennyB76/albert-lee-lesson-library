Problem 16 — Moon-Reflected Light & Photoelectric Effect

Student: Albert Lee Date: May 13, 2026
Problem 16 — Photon intensity, photon flux, photocurrent, work function, stopping potential. Companion piece to today's Lecture Note (Problems 7–15).
⚡ How to use this worksheet
Six parts (a → f) all share the same Setup. Solve each one in the dashed work-space first — don't peek at the green Solution box until you've tried. Final answers are boxed in green. Notice how part (a) → (b) → (c) → (d) is one continuous chain: each answer feeds the next.
Setup
The average earth–moon distance is $3.84 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m}$. Suppose the moon reflects light with an average wavelength of $550\,\text{nm}$. Suppose the intensity of this radiation on a given clear night, neglecting atmospheric losses, is $0.10\,\text{W/m}^{2}$.
Given: $d = 3.84\times10^{8}$ m,   $\lambda = 550$ nm,   $I = 0.10\,\text{W/m}^{2}$
Useful constants
$h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\,\text{J}\!\cdot\!\text{s}$  ·  $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$  ·  $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$  ·  $1\,\text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{J}$  ·  $hc \approx 1240\,\text{eV}\!\cdot\!\text{nm}$
Part (a) Average photon energy
Question
What is the average energy of the photons reflected by the moon?
Solution
Formula. $\;E = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}.$ Use the shortcut $hc = 1240\,\text{eV}\!\cdot\!\text{nm}.$
Substitute. $\;E = \dfrac{1240\,\text{eV}\!\cdot\!\text{nm}}{550\,\text{nm}} \approx 2.255\,\text{eV}.$
In joules. $\;E \approx 2.255 \times 1.602\!\times\!10^{-19} \approx 3.61\!\times\!10^{-19}\,\text{J}.$
$E \approx 2.26$ eV $\;\approx\; 3.61 \times 10^{-19}$ J
Part (b) Photons striking $1\,\text{m}^{2}$ on earth per second
Question
How many photons strike a square meter on earth per second?
Solution
Idea. Intensity = energy flux. Divide by energy-per-photon to get photon flux. $$\frac{N}{A\cdot t} \;=\; \frac{I}{E_{\text{photon}}}.$$
Substitute. $\;\dfrac{N}{(1\,\text{m}^2)(1\,\text{s})} = \dfrac{0.10\,\text{W/m}^{2}}{3.61\!\times\!10^{-19}\,\text{J}} \approx 2.77 \times 10^{17}\,\text{photons/m}^{2}\text{/s}.$
$N/(\text{m}^{2}\!\cdot\!\text{s}) \approx 2.77 \times 10^{17}$ photons
Scale check. A typical clear night → about $10^{17}$ visible photons hitting every square meter per second from the moon alone. Light is "lumpy," but the lumps are absurdly numerous.
Part (c) Photons striking a $1\,\text{cm}^{2}$ photocell per second
Question
How many photons strike a photocell, $1\,\text{cm}$ on a side, per second?
Solution
Convert area. $\;A = 1\,\text{cm}^{2} = (10^{-2}\,\text{m})^{2} = 10^{-4}\,\text{m}^{2}.$
Scale (b) by area. $\;N_{\text{cell}}/s = (2.77\!\times\!10^{17}) \times 10^{-4} \approx 2.77 \times 10^{13}\,\text{photons/s}.$
$N_{\text{cell}} \approx 2.77 \times 10^{13}$ photons/s
Part (d) Ideal-photocell photocurrent
Question
If the photocell is ideal (each photon causes one electron to be emitted), what is the photocurrent?
Solution
Idea. 1 photon → 1 electron, so the number of electrons per second equals the number of photons per second. Each electron carries charge $e$: $$I_{\text{cur}} \;=\; \frac{N_{\text{cell}}}{t}\cdot e.$$
Substitute. $\;I_{\text{cur}} = (2.77\!\times\!10^{13}) \times (1.602\!\times\!10^{-19}) \approx 4.44\!\times\!10^{-6}\,\text{A}.$
$I_{\text{cur}} \approx 4.44\,\mu\text{A}$
Unit sanity. $\text{photons/s} \times \text{C/photon} = \text{C/s} = \text{A}.$ Makes sense.
Part (e) Maximum allowable work function
Question
What is the maximum work function of the cell to emit this current?
Solution
Idea. For photoemission to occur, the photon must carry at least the work function's worth of energy: $\;hf \geq W_{0}.$ The maximum allowable $W_{0}$ is the case of equality — i.e., $W_{0}$ exactly equals the photon energy.
Plug in (a). $\;W_{0,\max} = E_{\text{photon}} \approx 2.26\,\text{eV} \approx 3.61 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}.$
$W_{0,\max} \approx 2.26$ eV $\;\approx\; 3.61\times10^{-19}$ J
What if $W_{0}$ is bigger? The photon would not have enough energy; no electrons ejected; $I_{\text{cur}} = 0.$
Part (f) Stopping voltage when $W = 1.0$ eV
Question
If the work function is $1.0\,\text{eV}$, what is the stopping voltage of the emitted electrons?
Solution
Einstein's equation. $\;KE_{\max} = hf - W_{0} = E_{\text{photon}} - W_{0}.$
Substitute. $\;KE_{\max} = 2.255\,\text{eV} - 1.0\,\text{eV} = 1.255\,\text{eV}.$
Stopping potential. $\;eV_{s} = KE_{\max} \Longrightarrow V_{s} = \dfrac{KE_{\max}}{e}.$ Numerically (in eV): $V_{s}$ in volts equals $KE_{\max}$ in eV.
$V_{s} \approx 1.26\,\text{V}$
Connection to the lab. If you ran an I–V sweep on this photocell, you would find the photocurrent drops to zero at $V \approx -1.26\,\text{V}.$ That cut-off point is the stopping voltage — it's how $V_{s}$ is measured experimentally.
